Roots in a Sewer Line: Repair Options in West Linn, OR

Tree roots find their way into sewer lines through joints and small cracks, drawn by moisture and nutrients in the pipe. Once inside, they grow into a mat that catches paper and grease and blocks the line.

This guide explains how roots get in, how a plumber clears them, how to stop them returning and when the pipe needs repair. For a backup now, stop water use and call (503) 974-3329.

By the West Linn 24/7 Plumbing team · Published November 13, 2026 · West Linn, OR

Call (503) 974-3329

How roots get in

A sewer pipe is a small, steady source of water and warmth, and roots grow toward it. A joint that has shifted, a hairline crack or an old clay pipe with loose seams gives them an opening. Roots enter as fine hairs, then thicken inside the pipe.

The result is a root mass that narrows the line. Toilet paper and grease snag on it, and the line clogs again and again.

Signs roots may be in your line

A pattern matters more than one event.

  • Slow drains that return a few weeks after each clearing.
  • Gurgling in toilets when other fixtures drain.
  • A backup in the lowest drain after heavy rain.
  • Mature trees or large shrubs near the lateral.
  • An older house with clay or cast iron pipe.

Clearing the roots

The plumber feeds a cable with a cutting head down the line and chops the root mass. A hydro jetter follows to scour the pipe and flush out debris. Both clear the line, but they do not repair the entry point.

Roots grow back, sometimes within months. A camera after clearing shows how many entry points there are and whether the pipe is sound.

Preventing a repeat

The best prevention is a tight pipe. Roots cannot enter a sealed joint. Lining or replacement removes the opening. Where that is not planned, a cleaning every one to two years, timed before the growing season, keeps small roots from becoming a mass.

Avoid planting willows, maples and other thirsty trees near the lateral. Some root killers sold for toilets can harm pipes and the environment, so ask the plumber before using one.

Roots, the city main and who to call

The City of West Linn's sanitary sewer page says the Sewer Branch works with the Stormwater Branch as part of the Environmental Service Division. For backups, the city sewer FAQ lists the Public Works Operations Division at 503-722-5500 during business hours and LOCOM non-emergency dispatch at 503-635-0238 after hours.

Roots can enter the city main as well as your lateral. If neighbors on your street see backups at the same time, the city's pipe may be involved, and a report to Public Works helps. The city's history page shows the population rising from 2,923 in 1960 to over 7,000 in 1970, so many older laterals were laid in that growth and are now sixty or more years old.

Repair or replace: what the camera decides

If the camera shows a few entry points on sound pipe, lining or spot repair may solve the problem. If it shows a collapsed or severely offset pipe, replacement is the lasting answer. The plumber explains the options and the price of each, and you decide.

Our sewer line repair page lists the repair routes, and sewer inspection covers the camera. A repair that needs digging in a street or right of way may need a permit, so ask the City of West Linn Building Division at 503-656-4211.

Nearby towns we serve

Homes in Oregon City and Gladstone with mature trees see the same root pattern. The sequence is the same: clear the line, camera it, then choose the repair.

What to ask the plumber

Ask where on the line the roots were found and how far from the house. Ask whether the pipe is clay, cast iron or plastic and whether the camera showed a sag or an offset. Ask for the recording and for written options with prices.

A good answer includes a recommendation and a reason. If the plumber suggests digging, ask why lining or spot repair would not work. If lining is suggested, ask whether the pipe is sound enough for it. The questions take a few minutes and often save a repeat visit.

Finally, ask what to watch for. A good plumber will tell you which symptoms mean the roots are back, such as a gurgle or a slow tub, and how soon to call. Write the date of the visit on the cleanout cap with a marker, so you can see how long the line stayed clear.

Plumbing work in Oregon follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). This guide is general information. For your own house, ask the plumber which rules apply and check permits with the City of West Linn Building Division.
