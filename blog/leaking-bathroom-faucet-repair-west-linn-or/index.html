How to Repair a Leaking Bathroom Faucet: A West Linn, OR Guide

A bathroom faucet can leak from the spout, around the handles or underneath the sink. Each location points to a different part, and finding the right one first saves time and parts.

Below is how to trace the leak, what to replace and when to stop and call. For a supply line or valve that will not close, close the main and call (503) 974-3329.

Trace the leak before you take anything apart

Dry the sink and the cabinet, then run the faucet and watch. Water at the spout tip while the faucet is off points to the cartridge or washer. Water at the base of the handle points to O-rings. Water on the cabinet floor under the sink points to the supply lines, the stop valves or the drain.

Dry a paper towel against each connection under the sink and look for the damp one. A leak that starts only when the water is on is a supply issue, while a leak that continues after shutoff may be the faucet.

What to check under the sink

Do this before blaming the faucet.

  • Feel each supply line connection for moisture.
  • Check the stop valve stems for drips.
  • Look at the drain trap and the pop-up assembly.
  • Check the cabinet floor for swelling or stains.
  • Look for green or white crust on copper fittings.

Repairing the faucet itself

Most bathroom faucets use cartridges or ceramic discs. Shut off both stops, remove the handle and replace the cartridge or the worn O-rings with an exact match. Clean the seat while the faucet is open, and test slowly. A pitted seat or a corroded body means replacement is the better value.

A widespread faucet with separate hot and cold handles has two cartridges, and both usually need replacing together. A faucet that is more than fifteen or twenty years old, with a worn finish, is often not worth chasing.

Why copper stains and green crust matter

A blue or green stain on the sink points to copper dissolving from a pipe or fixture, and persistent moisture speeds it. The stain is a signal that something is leaking or running, not just a cosmetic issue. Fix the leak first, then clean the stain.

What West Linn's report says about stains and screens

The City of West Linn's water quality report says copper stains on fixtures can come from leaky faucets or faulty toilet flush valves, and tells residents to check their plumbing. The 2024 results list lead and copper at the 90th percentile as not detected, with no sites above the action level.

That is good news for the city supply, and it points the finger at your own fixtures when you see a stain. The report also advises cleaning faucet screens and aerators regularly. A clogged aerator can make a faucet spit or leak at the spout tip, so clean it before replacing parts.

When to call a plumber

Call if a stop valve will not shut off, if a supply line is corroded, if the leak is in the wall or if you cannot match the parts. Our faucet repair page lists what we handle, and a clear price comes before work starts.

If water is spraying or the sink cabinet is already soaked, close the main valve first. The sooner the water is stopped, the less drywall and flooring you lose.

Supply lines and stop valves: the cheap fix that saves the cabinet

Many bathroom leaks are not the faucet at all. Braided supply lines can fray, and compression nuts can loosen as the cabinet is used for storage. A stop valve that has not been turned in years can start to weep at the stem. Replacing a supply line is a quick, low cost job, and replacing an old stop while the line is off prevents a second call.

Lay a paper towel under each connection overnight. The wet one tells you where to look. If the particleboard floor of the cabinet has swollen, dry it out, because wet particleboard breaks down and holds moisture against the pipe.
