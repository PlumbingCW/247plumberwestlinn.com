Old bluff and river housing, and what it means for pipes

Oregon City is built on two levels. The city's own history of its Municipal Elevator describes the early town on the first level along the Willamette River, with the bluff above it. That split matters for plumbing. Homes at the river level and homes on the bluff sit at different elevations, which changes water pressure and how sewer lines drain.

Houses in the older parts of town predate modern pipe standards. A home with galvanized supply lines or clay sewer pipe is more likely to show leaks, rust-colored water and repeat drain clogs. If your house is in that group, a camera look through sewer inspection shows what the line really looks like.

Water in Oregon City

The city's water page states that Oregon City's drinking water comes from the Clackamas River, treated by the South Fork Water Board and then bought and distributed by the city. West Linn buys from the same board, so the source water is shared across the river. That is useful when you compare a leak or a taste complaint with a West Linn neighbor.

The city page gives no hardness figure, so we do not quote one. If you want to know what is in your tap, the city publishes an annual water quality report.

The Municipal Elevator and the two-level city

Oregon City's Municipal Elevator connects the lower downtown to the bluff. The city says the original elevator opened on November 27, 1915, and the current 130 foot elevator was dedicated May 5, 1955. The ride is free, and the city describes it as one of only four municipal elevators in the world.

What this means for plumbing: the drop from bluff to river is real, so pressure at a lower house can run higher than at a house on top. If a faucet or fill valve keeps wearing out in a low lying home, ask the plumber to check pressure at a hose bib.
